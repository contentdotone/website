Why an RFP at all

A good website RFP saves more than it costs to write

Issuing an RFP for a website redesign feels like overhead. It isn't. A structured website RFP forces your team to align internally — on scope, on success metrics, on must-haves vs nice-to-haves — *before* you ever talk to a vendor. That alignment alone is usually worth more than the proposals you'll receive.

1. It gets your stakeholders to commit on paper

The CMO has a vision. The CFO has a number. IT has a security posture. The communications lead has language standards. A website redesign RFP makes everyone sign off on the same document — so when the vendor builds exactly what was asked for, no one says "but I meant something different" in week six.

2. It exposes the cheap bidders

Without a clear scope, the cheapest proposal always looks the most attractive — until you realize it didn't include content migration, accessibility audits, or post-launch support. A real website design RFP template prices the same scope across vendors, so you're comparing apples.

3. It surfaces governance gaps early

If you operate sites across regions, franchises, or program areas, the CMS section of your RFP is where multi-location complexity becomes visible. Skipping it means you discover the gap during implementation — when it costs ten times as much to fix.

4. It documents the decision

Two years from now, when someone asks why you chose the vendor you did, the RFP and the scored evaluation rubric are the answer. That's not just CYA — it's the institutional memory that helps the next person make a better call.

What to avoid

Five mistakes that sink website RFPs

Patterns we've seen across hundreds of website RFP examples — from Fortune 500 portfolios to non-profits running their first redesign in a decade.

  1. 1

    Padding the page count instead of the scope

    A fifty-page RFP for website redesign isn't more serious than a ten-page one. Vendors skim. Lead with scope and outcomes; relegate boilerplate (NDA, payment terms, indemnity) to appendices.

  2. 2

    Asking for "modern, mobile-friendly" without saying what that means

    Vague aesthetic requirements turn into "the agency picked something I hate." Anchor your design language section in two or three reference sites and the *brand attributes* you want them to embody.

  3. 3

    Bundling the CMS into "miscellaneous tech"

    The CMS choice outlives the redesign. Make it its own section: headless vs hybrid, SSO + roles, multi-site governance, audit trails, AI editing model, content modeling. An informed RFP content management system section pre-empts the painful procurement re-runs that happen when the chosen vendor's CMS can't actually do what marketing needs in year two.

  4. 4

    Skipping the post-launch section

    Most website redesign RFP templates stop at launch. The real cost shows up in the next 24 months — content updates, accessibility re-tests, performance regressions, new campaigns. Specify the post-launch support model up front or budget for surprise change orders.

  5. 5

    Letting the evaluation drift to "vibes"

    A weighted rubric — scope coverage, multi-site experience, CMS depth, references, cost, team chemistry — keeps the committee honest. Without one, the loudest voice wins. The template includes the rubric so you don't have to build it from scratch.

Frequently asked questions

Common questions teams ask before they start drafting an RFP for a website redesign.

What is a website redesign RFP template?

A website redesign RFP template is a structured document you send to vendors when you're soliciting bids for a website rebuild. It sets out the project's goals, scope, technical requirements, timeline, budget, and how proposals will be evaluated — so the vendors you talk to bid on the same thing and you can compare them like-for-like. The template above is a starting point; you edit it to match your organization and your project.

What should a website RFP include?

At minimum: project background and goals, scope of work, CMS and technical requirements, timeline and milestones, budget structure, a vendor questionnaire, and a weighted evaluation rubric. If you operate multi-location or multi-brand sites, a dedicated governance section is essential — it's where most website redesign projects under-scope and overspend.

How do you write an RFP for a website redesign?

Start with internal alignment: pull the CMO, the CFO, IT, and a content lead into a room and agree on three things — the business outcome the redesign needs to drive, what's in scope vs out, and a realistic budget range. Then drop those answers into the template, fill out the technical and CMS requirements, write 8–12 questions you want every vendor to answer, set a submission deadline, and email it to 4–6 vetted vendors. Most teams complete the draft in two working sessions plus revisions.

What's the difference between a website RFP and a website design RFP?

A website RFP typically covers the full project — design, development, content migration, hosting, and CMS — and goes to full-service vendors. A website design RFP is narrower: it's for engagements where you've already chosen your CMS and developer, and you're just sourcing visual + UX design. The template here handles both — use the full version for the broader engagement, or strip the technical and hosting sections for a pure design solicitation.

Can you share website RFP examples?

The template itself is the example — it's the structure we've seen produce strong vendor responses across enterprise non-profits, retail brands, media properties, and B2B SaaS. We don't redistribute individual customer RFPs (those are confidential), but the section structure, questionnaire, and rubric are lifted from the real ones that worked.

How long should a website redesign RFP be?

Eight to fifteen pages of substantive content, plus appendices. Anything shorter doesn't give vendors enough to bid against. Anything longer signals indecision and pads vendor pricing. The template lands in that range when filled out fully.

What should an RFP for a content management system include?

An RFP content management system section should cover: architecture (headless / hybrid / coupled), hosting and uptime SLA, SSO and role-based access, audit trails, content modeling, multi-site and multi-locale governance, AI / generative-content controls, API surface, accessibility tooling, migration support, and total cost of ownership. The template lays these out as checkboxes and short-answer prompts so vendors can't dodge them.

How does the template adapt for multi-location organizations?

There's a dedicated section pre-written for multi-location and federated-site organizations — chapters, regions, franchise locations, program areas. It asks vendors to address central brand governance, local editing rights, content reuse across locations, and how the proposed CMS handles rollups + per-location overrides. This section is what most generic website RFP templates skip and what most multi-site redesigns under-scope.
