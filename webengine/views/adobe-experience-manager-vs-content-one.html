Adobe Experience Manager vs Content.One: two different bets on scale

Adobe Experience Manager is Adobe's enterprise content and asset management platform, and it's genuinely strong at what it's built for. If you're already deep in Adobe Experience Cloud (Analytics, Target, Real-Time CDP, Adobe Commerce) and you've got a Java-capable development team on staff or on retainer, AEM slots into that ecosystem cleanly. It shows up on G2's list of top content management software for a reason.

The catch is what it takes to get there. AEM doesn't publish pricing. You talk to Adobe sales, and depending on which modules you need (Sites, Assets, Forms, Screens), you're looking at license costs anywhere from $30,000 a year for a single module up to $200,000+ for a multi-module enterprise deployment, plus implementation that typically runs 2 to 4 times that license cost in year one. A $60,000/year AEM Sites deployment commonly books $120,000 to $240,000 of partner implementation before the team even takes over day-to-day operation.

Content.One takes the opposite approach for a specific kind of buyer: franchise networks, multi-location service organizations, and federated nonprofits that need governed, on-brand content across dozens or thousands of properties, but don't have the budget or headcount for a permanent AEM implementation team.

Where Content.One takes the lead

The honest version of this comparison isn't "AEM is bad." It's that AEM was built for a different buyer. If you're running a single, large, complex enterprise site with a dedicated development team, the platform's depth makes sense. Most franchise networks, dental service organizations, and multi-chapter nonprofits aren't that buyer. They're marketing-led, they've got dozens to thousands of nearly-identical properties, and they need a location manager to update hours or a promo without opening a ticket.

That's where the gap shows up in practice. AEM's own reviewers, even the ones giving it high marks, keep naming the same two issues: the learning curve and the cost. Content.One's agentic page builder exists specifically to remove the developer bottleneck from routine publishing. Internal benchmarks put a typical enterprise page build at about an hour with the agent handling the scaffolding, down from roughly 32 hours in a legacy component-based CMS like AEM.

Cost is the other half of it. AEM's real number isn't the license fee Adobe quotes you, it's the license plus 2 to 4× that in implementation, plus the ongoing Java development team to maintain it. Content.One's Engineer on Demand model exists so migrations and custom work happen on an as-needed basis instead of requiring a standing team.

Where AEM fits

If you're already running Adobe Analytics, Adobe Target, Real-Time CDP, and Adobe Commerce, and your organization has the internal Java expertise (or budget for a certified Solution Partner) to run a multi-year implementation, AEM's tight integration across the Adobe Experience Cloud is a real advantage that's hard to replicate outside Adobe's ecosystem. It's also a defensible choice for organizations managing genuinely complex, high-volume digital asset libraries where AEM Assets' maturity matters more than publishing speed. If that's your situation and the budget's already allocated, AEM's depth is legitimate, not just legacy inertia.

Frequently asked questions

The questions that come up most often when teams compare AEM and Content.One.

What happens if we're still on AEM 6.5 after August 2026?

Adobe ends support for AEM 6.5 LTS on August 31, 2026. After that date, you're running an unsupported on-prem system, which means no security patches and no official help if something breaks. Organizations still on 6.5 need a migration plan now, whether that's to AEM as a Cloud Service or an alternative platform.

Why doesn't Adobe publish AEM pricing?

AEM pricing is modular and negotiated per organization based on which components you need (Sites, Assets, Forms, Screens) and your usage volume. Adobe sends every prospect to a sales conversation rather than a pricing page, which is standard for enterprise DXP but makes it hard to budget without already talking to them.

Do we need Java developers to run Content.One the way we'd need them for AEM?

No. Content.One's agentic page builder lets marketers build and edit pages without touching code. Engineer on Demand covers anything that genuinely needs custom development, on an as-needed basis rather than a permanent headcount requirement.

Can Content.One migrate an existing AEM instance?

Yes. The migration.content.one tool assesses your current AEM setup and gives you a timeline and cost estimate, and Engineer on Demand handles the actual migration work.

Is AEM overkill for a franchise or multi-location network?

Often, yes. AEM's Multi-Site Manager can technically support a multi-location structure, but it's built for organizations with the development resources to configure and maintain that complexity. Most franchise and multi-location teams are marketing-led, not engineering-led, which is the gap Content.One is built to close.

Does Content.One handle digital asset management the way AEM Assets does?

Content.One includes asset management built into the same platform, though AEM Assets remains the more mature option for organizations with very large, complex digital asset libraries as their primary need rather than website publishing.
