Drupal vs Content.One: how they actually differ

Let's give Drupal its due, because it earns it. It's 25 years old, it powers a huge share of government, higher-education, and nonprofit sites, and its security reputation is one of the best in the category. Multilingual is a core strength, the contributed module library is enormous, and on January 28, 2026 Drupal shipped one of its biggest releases ever. Drupal CMS 2.0 added Drupal Canvas for on-page visual editing, AI tools, and site templates, so marketing teams can launch branded sites in days rather than weeks. It's free, open-source, and you own your data outright. For a team with developers, that's a serious platform.

Here's the part that doesn't show up on a feature list. "Free" is the license, not the cost. You host Drupal yourself or pay a specialist host like Acquia or Pantheon, you apply the security patches, and you carry the core and module upgrades. Beyond a starter template, most real builds still run through developers or an agency. None of that is a knock on Drupal. It's the deal you sign up for with self-hosted open-source, and for an engineering team that wants full control, it's a fair trade. For a lean marketing team running 40 chapter sites, it's a standing cost that never really ends.

Multisite is a good example. Drupal multisite is native and genuinely capable, but it's a shared-codebase model you run yourself. Every site rides the same code, and one upgrade or security patch is a job across all of them. That works when you have the people to manage it. It gets heavy when you don't.

This is where Content.One is built differently, and it's worth being specific. Content.One includes hosting and global CDN delivery, so there are no servers to run or patch. It's designed around federated multisite, so one team runs many sites and brands from a single managed platform instead of maintaining a shared Drupal codebase. Publishing is visual and no-ticket, SSO, custom roles, and audit logs sit in the standard product, and there's a native MCP server so AI agents can work with content directly. The upgrades and patching that eat a Drupal team's week are simply not your job.

Reviews track the split. Content.One's reviews currently sit under its former name, Zesty.io, on G2 and Capterra, since the rebrand is still rolling out, and the recurring Capterra themes are ease of use for non-developers and hands-on support, with one customer saying the team "goes above and beyond." On the Drupal side, reviewers praise the flexibility, the security, and the multilingual depth, and the most common caveats are just as steady: a steep learning curve, complex setup, and performance that needs tuning and extra infrastructure as sites grow.

None of this makes Drupal the wrong call. If you want open-source control, full data ownership, deep custom functionality from the module library, and you have the developers or agency to run it, Drupal is an excellent choice, and Drupal CMS 2.0 closes a real chunk of the old ease-of-use gap. The honest decision is about who carries the platform. If you have an engineering team and want to own every layer, Drupal fits. If you want the same federated multisite reach without hosting, patching, and upgrades on your plate, that's the case for Content.One. (Comparing the headless names too? Here's Contentful vs Content.One and Sanity vs Content.One.)

When Drupal is the better pick

Drupal is the stronger choice if you want a free, open-source platform with full code and data ownership and no vendor lock-in, you have or will hire the developers or agency to build and maintain it, you need deep custom functionality from the contributed module library, multilingual at scale is core to your sites, or your security and compliance posture favors self-hosting with complete control.

Frequently asked questions

The questions that come up most often when teams compare Drupal and Content.One.

Is Content.One a good Drupal alternative?

For multi-site teams that want federated reach without hosting and maintaining the platform themselves, yes. Drupal is the stronger pick if you want open-source control and have the developers to run it.

Is Drupal free?

The software is free and open-source, so there's no license fee. The real cost is Drupal hosting, developers or an agency to build and maintain the site, and the time spent on updates. Content.One is quote-based with hosting and maintenance included.

Does Drupal include hosting?

No. You self-host Drupal or pay a specialist host such as Acquia, Pantheon, or Platform.sh. Content.One includes hosting and global CDN delivery.

Do I need a developer to use Drupal?

For setup and anything beyond a starter template, usually yes. Drupal CMS 2.0 added Canvas for on-page editing and site templates, which helps marketers, but modules, theming, and maintenance still lean on developers. Content.One is built so a non-developer can publish from the start.

How does Drupal multisite compare to Content.One?

Drupal multisite is native but runs on a shared codebase you host and maintain, so updates and patches apply across every site. Content.One offers managed federated multisite, where one team runs many sites from a single platform without the codebase upkeep.

Does Drupal support AI agents and MCP?

Yes. Drupal CMS 2.0 ships AI tools, and Drupal can act as an MCP server through contributed modules like mcp_server and MCP Tools. Those are community-maintained and still pre-stable, and you install and configure them. Content.One ships a native MCP server.

Is migrating from Drupal to Content.One hard?

Effort depends on how many sites, content types, and languages you're moving. You can get an estimate from the migration tool at migration.content.one.

Why are Content.One reviews listed under Zesty.io?

Content.One was rebranded from Zesty.io, and the review profiles on G2 and Capterra haven't fully migrated yet.
