Why a nonprofit-specific template

Why nonprofit website RFPs need their own template

Generic RFP templates assume one organization, one website, and a paid content team. Most nonprofits have none of those things. You have chapters or affiliates who want their own pages, volunteers who need to publish without breaking anything, a board that wants to see the numbers before it signs, and accessibility obligations that a corporate template never mentions.

1. Federated ownership breaks the "one team owns the site" assumption

In a federated organization, national owns the brand, chapters own their local content, and nobody has agreed in writing who can publish what. If the RFP does not settle that question, the vendor guesses, and you find out which way they guessed after launch.

2. Volunteers editing on a Tuesday evening is a real requirement

Corporate templates ask about the CMS as a technical requirement. For a nonprofit, the real question is whether a volunteer chapter coordinator can update an event listing without a support ticket. That belongs in the RFP, and this template puts it there.

3. Accessibility is far cheaper to specify than to remediate

Nonprofits serving the public carry obligations that a marketing brochure site does not. There is a WCAG section for it, with language you can hand directly to a vendor.

4. Boards and grant committees want a defensible decision

Two years from now, when someone asks why you chose the vendor you did, the RFP and the scored evaluation rubric are the answer. That is not just documentation, it is the institutional memory that helps the next person make a better call.

Associations and membership orgs

Trade associations, alumni networks & membership bodies

These organizations run into a version of the same problem, with member data attached. An association website RFP has to cover member authentication, gated content tiers, dues and renewals, event registration, and how the website talks to the AMS. Generic templates skip all of it.

The template includes those sections. If you are running an association rather than a charity, the chapter governance sections map onto regions or special interest groups without changes.

Frequently asked questions

Common questions teams ask before they start drafting a nonprofit website RFP.

Is the nonprofit website RFP template really free?

Yes. Enter an email address and it arrives as an editable document you can adapt however you like.

How long should a nonprofit website RFP be?

Eight to fifteen pages covers almost every project. Longer RFPs do not produce better proposals, they produce longer ones. The template lands in that range once filled in.

Do we need an RFP at all?

If a board, a grant condition, or a procurement policy requires competitive bids, yes. For smaller projects a scoped brief and three conversations are often faster and get you to the same place.

Does this work for a multi-chapter or federated organization?

That is what it was written for. Chapter governance, local publishing permissions, and shared brand control each get their own section, because those are the requirements corporate templates leave out.

Can we use it for an association or membership site?

Yes. Member authentication, gated content, dues, and AMS integration are covered, and the chapter sections map onto regions or interest groups.

What budget range should we put in the RFP?

Put in a real one. Leaving it out does not get you cheaper proposals, it gets you proposals scoped at random. A range is enough, and the template explains how to set one.

Should the RFP name a CMS?

Name your requirements rather than a product. Say what editors need to do without a developer, how many sites or chapters you run, and what has to integrate. Let vendors propose the platform against that.
